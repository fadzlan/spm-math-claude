# Form 5, Chapter 8: Mathematical Modelling

## Overview
Applies the mathematical modelling cycle to real situations using the three prescribed core model families: **linear, quadratic and exponential functions**. Students identify and define a problem, make assumptions, define variables, formulate and solve a model, interpret and validate the result, refine the model where necessary, and communicate the complete modelling process. Other mathematical structures may appear as explicitly tagged cross-topic enrichment, but must not displace these three core families.

## Prerequisites
Linear equations and straight lines (Form 1 Chapter 6 and Form 3 Chapter 9); indices (Form 3 Chapter 1); quadratic functions and equations within the methods taught in Form 4 Chapter 1; reading tables and graphs; units, rounding and calculator use.

## Difficulty strategies

### Core tasks
- **Easy**: use a given linear, quadratic or exponential model to calculate a value and interpret it with units; identify variables, assumptions or the model family.
- **Medium**: construct a model from a description or clean table; justify the family using constant differences, constant second differences or constant ratios; solve within a stated domain; validate against one unused datum and report one limitation.
- **Hard**: carry out the full cycle for one of the three core families; compare two plausible core models over a stated range; interpret multiple mathematical results and reject invalid ones; refine an assumption or parameter and communicate how that change affects the conclusion.
- Difficulty should come from modelling decisions, representation shifts, validation and communication—not from importing untaught algebra or enrichment content.

### Enrichment tasks
- Variation, geometric optimisation, network selection, residual comparisons, cylinders and inequality-region optimisation must be visibly tagged `scope: enrichment` and name their cross-topic prerequisites.
- Provide the operational method when it is not part of the core prerequisite: for example, a finite candidate list, a trial table, a graph, a residual definition, or the feasible-region vertices.
- Do not describe a numerical search as an exact continuous maximum or minimum. Phrase the conclusion as “best among the tested values” or “best to the stated increment” unless an appropriate proof is supplied.

### Common distractors and failure modes
- Omitting units or the meaning of the answer; confusing initial value with rate; using equal differences for exponential data or equal ratios for linear data.
- Accepting negative lengths/times, a fractional number of indivisible objects, or predictions outside the stated domain without discussion.
- Applying a percentage change additively instead of repeatedly; mismatching the growth rate and compounding interval; rounding intermediate values too early.
- Optimising the wrong quantity, ignoring a constraint, or claiming that a model is validated merely because it fits the data used to construct it.

## Visual aids
- **RECOMMENDED**: well-labelled tables and graphs for all three core families. Use equal input intervals when students infer constant differences, second differences or ratios; label axes, units, domain and whether values are observed or predicted.
- Context diagrams may support formulation, but must agree with the variable definitions and equations. Enrichment diagrams such as garden layouts, open-box nets, cylinder dimensions, networks or feasible regions must be tagged with their prerequisite topic.
- A report task may combine prose, equations, a table and a graph; every visual must have a text-equivalent data model, and the answer key must be regenerated from the same parameters.

## Generator notes
- Every core set should sample linear, quadratic and exponential functions. Do not treat variation, geometry or networks as a fourth prescribed core family.
- Attach `scope: core|enrichment` and the relevant learning-standard/prerequisite metadata to each item. Generate the mathematical instance first, solve independently, then render the context and report components.
- For family-selection items, create data from the intended model before rounding. After rounding, verify that the diagnostic pattern remains sufficiently clear and that no competing family fits equally well at the expected precision.
- State domains, units, time steps, compounding conventions and rounding rules. Validate every proposed answer against both the equation and the context; specify whether interpolation or extrapolation is being used.
- Reporting is part of the assessed modelling process: require some combination of model-choice justification, contextual interpretation, validation, limitation/refinement and conclusion rather than calculation alone.
- Break-even or plan-comparison templates may use linear intersections. Quadratic templates must use solution methods that are available in the stated prerequisites; do not silently require completing the square, the quadratic formula, calculus or a vertex formula.
- An open box cut from a square sheet has `V(x) = x(s - 2x)²` with `0 < x < s/2`. This is a **cubic**, not a quadratic. It is enrichment and must be accompanied by a supplied finite candidate set, trial table or stated step-size search. The answer is the greatest tested value (or an approximation at the stated resolution), not an exact maximum unless an appropriate proof method is explicitly supplied.
- For a closed cylinder of fixed volume `K`, substituting `h = K/(πr²)` into surface area gives `S(r) = 2πr² + 2K/r` for `r > 0`; an open cylinder has a different formula. Continuous minimisation is enrichment and is not justified by “calculus-free” inspection alone. Supply a finite set/table/graph and report the best tested radius at the stated precision, or explicitly provide an appropriate advanced method. State whether the cylinder is open or closed and include all required surfaces.
- Residual work is enrichment: define residual and the comparison criterion within the item, retain residual signs, and do not claim that the smallest errors alone establish causal validity.
- Inequality optimisation is enrichment: either provide the feasible region/vertices or explicitly cross-link Form 4 Chapter 6, and limit conclusions to the stated objective and feasible set.
- Keep values manageable at the model level. Context richness should arise from assumptions, interpretation, validation and reporting rather than unnecessarily difficult arithmetic.
- Cross-link: Form 3 Chapter 9 (straight lines), Form 4 Chapter 1 (quadratic functions), Form 3 Chapter 1 (indices), Form 5 Chapter 1 (variation, enrichment here), Form 4 Chapter 5 (networks, enrichment), Form 4 Chapter 6 (inequality regions, enrichment), and mensuration chapters (geometric/cylinder enrichment).
