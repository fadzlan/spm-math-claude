# Form 2, Chapter 12: Measures of Central Tendencies

## Overview
Introduces mean, median and mode for ungrouped and grouped data. Students collect and organise data, construct and interpret grouped frequency tables, identify the modal class, and estimate the mean of grouped data using class midpoints. The chapter also develops the effects of data changes, selection of a suitable measure in the presence of extreme values, comparison of data sets using central tendency and range, and evidence-based predictions and arguments. Continues the statistics line from Form 1 Ch12 toward Form 4 Ch8 dispersion.

## Prerequisites
Form 1 Ch12 (data representations); fraction/decimal arithmetic.

## Topic & subtopic breakdown

| # | Subtopic | Content coverage | Key skills / formulae | Typical question types |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12.1 | Data collection and organisation | Form a statistical question; identify the population or group of interest; collect suitable primary or secondary data; record observations consistently; choose sensible, non-overlapping class intervals; tally and construct a grouped frequency table; interpret frequencies and totals. | Frequency = number of observations in a class; class intervals must cover every observation exactly once. | Plan a short data collection; complete a tally/frequency table; group a raw data set; detect a gap, overlap, inconsistent unit or incorrect total in a table. |
| 12.2 | Measures of central tendencies | Mode (most frequent value, incl. bimodal/no mode); median (middle of ordered data, average of two middle values for even n); mean (sum ÷ count); compute from raw lists, ungrouped frequency tables, pictographs, bar charts, stem-and-leaf and dot plots; compare the measures. | Mean = Σx/n or Σfx/Σf; median position for odd n = (n+1)/2. | Compute mode/median/mean from a list or representation; find a missing value from a stated mean; find mean from a chart or ungrouped frequency table. |
| 12.3 | Grouped data | Interpret a grouped frequency table; determine the modal class; find each class midpoint; use midpoints as representative values to estimate the mean. The exact mean generally cannot be recovered from grouped intervals because the individual observations are unknown. | Midpoint x = (lower endpoint + upper endpoint)/2; **estimated mean** = Σfx/Σf, where x is the class midpoint. | Identify the modal class; complete midpoint and fx columns; calculate an estimated mean; construct or interpret a grouped frequency table in context. |
| 12.4 | Choosing, predicting and comparing | Select and justify the most suitable measure: mode for the most common category/value, median when extreme values distort the mean, and mean when all numerical values should contribute; recognise that suitability depends on context and distribution. Compare two or more data sets using an appropriate central tendency together with range; make predictions and convincing arguments supported by the data, while avoiding claims beyond the sample. | Range = maximum − minimum; compare both a typical value and spread rather than using one statistic alone. | Explain why median is preferable to mean when an extreme value is present; compare consistency and typical performance of data sets; predict or argue from a table/chart and cite numerical evidence; critique an unsupported conclusion. |
| 12.5 | Effects of data changes | Effect of adding/removing a data value on mean/median/mode and range; finding a missing value from the mean (Σx = mean × n); effect of multiplying/adding a constant to every value; reverse problems (new mean after adding k). | New sum = old sum ± change; if every value becomes ax+b, the new mean is a(old mean)+b; for a>0, the new range is a(old range). | Find missing x given mean; find a new mean after adding a value; decide which measures and whether the range change when an extreme value is added or removed. |

## Difficulty strategies

### 12.1
- **Easy**: complete tallies or frequencies from a short raw list; check that the frequencies total the number of observations.
- **Medium**: choose suitable class intervals and construct a grouped frequency table from raw data; explain what one class frequency means in context.
- **Hard**: plan a data collection for a stated statistical question and justify the population, variable, unit and grouping; diagnose a table with overlapping/gapped classes or inconsistent recording.
- Validation: specify interval-boundary conventions unambiguously; ensure every generated observation belongs to exactly one class; do not imply that a small or biased sample represents a wider population.

### 12.2
- **Easy**: mode of a short list; median of an odd-n ordered list; mean of 4–5 small integers with a clean result.
- **Medium**: median of an even-n list (needs averaging); mean from an ungrouped frequency table; mean from a bar chart/pictograph (extract frequencies first); median from a stem-and-leaf plot; non-integer mean with a rounding instruction.
- **Hard**: combined representations (frequencies split across two charts); weighted-mean style combined groups (class A mean 70 from 20 students, class B mean 80 from 30 — combined mean ≠ 75).
- Distractors: forgetting to order values before finding the median; dividing Σfx by Σf incorrectly; treating frequencies as data values; averaging two group means without accounting for unequal group sizes.

### 12.3
- **Easy**: identify the modal class from a grouped frequency table; calculate given class midpoints.
- **Medium**: complete midpoint and fx columns, then calculate the estimated mean; interpret a class frequency or compare two modal classes.
- **Hard**: group a raw data set and then estimate its mean; complete a grouped table with a missing frequency from a stated total or estimated mean; compare the exact mean of raw data with the estimate after grouping and explain the difference.
- Required language: call Σfx/Σf an **estimated mean** whenever x is a class midpoint. Do not label it as the exact mean unless all observations are explicitly stated to equal their class midpoints.
- Validation: use ordered, exhaustive, non-overlapping intervals and midpoints consistent with their endpoints; ensure Σf matches the stated sample size and Σfx is recomputed from the rendered table.

### 12.4
- **Easy**: choose a suitable measure for a familiar context and give one relevant reason; calculate the range of a data set.
- **Medium**: compare two data sets using a central tendency and range; explain how an extreme value affects the suitability of mean versus median; support a prediction with values from a table or chart.
- **Hard**: compare three data sets or two differently shaped distributions; construct or evaluate a convincing argument using more than one statistic; identify that similar means can conceal different ranges or that a lower range indicates greater consistency, not automatically better performance.
- Argument prompts must identify the claim and require numerical evidence. Accept different conclusions when they are mathematically supported by an appropriate statistic and the context.

### 12.5
- **Easy**: new mean when a value equal to current mean is added (unchanged — conceptual).
- **Medium**: find missing value x given mean of n numbers (mean 12 of 5 numbers with sum partially given).
- **Hard**: mean changes from m₁ to m₂ when a value is added/removed — find the value; effect of removing an outlier on mean, median and range (reasoning); two-step (find x, then find new median); scaling (every value ×2 + 3 → new mean) recognition.
- Distractors: solving x with wrong n; assuming median changes whenever mean changes; additive scaling applied twice.

## Visual aids
- **REQUIRED** when data is given via a graphical representation — bar chart, pictograph (with key), dot plot or stem-and-leaf plot. Frequency and grouped frequency tables may be rendered accessibly as text/HTML tables; pure list questions need no visual.
- Keep the displayed representation and the answer key derived from the same underlying data. Include titles, labels, units, keys and accessible text equivalents where relevant.

## Generator notes
- For ungrouped frequency-table means, pick frequencies so Σfx is divisible cleanly at Easy/Medium; require a stated rounding rule (for example, "correct to 2 d.p.") when needed.
- For grouped tables, generate the raw observations or class model first, then derive class frequencies, midpoints and fx values. Always label the result **estimated mean** and state the required degree of accuracy when it does not terminate.
- Missing-value questions: choose mean and values first, derive the missing one — guarantees integer.
- Combined-group mean questions are a reliable Hard SPM item.
- Measure-choice questions must include contextual features that make the justification meaningful (for example, a genuine extreme value, categorical data, or a need to include every value), rather than asking for a memorised rule.
- Comparison questions should normally provide both a measure of central tendency and range, or enough data to calculate them. Do not infer that one group is universally "better" from range alone: describe range as spread/consistency and interpret direction using the context.
- Prediction and argument questions must distinguish a conclusion about the observed sample from a generalisation to a population; provide sampling information if a wider inference is intended.
