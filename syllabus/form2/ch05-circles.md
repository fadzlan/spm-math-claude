# Form 2, Chapter 5: Circles

## Overview
Properties, constructions and mensuration of circles: parts of a circle, symmetrical properties of chords, circumference, arc length, area, sector area, and related measurements. Formulae for circumference and area are developed through investigation before they are applied. This chapter supports Form 3 Chapter 6 (angles and tangents of circles) and the use of arcs and sectors in three-dimensional-shape problems.

## Prerequisites
Form 1 Chapter 8 (lines, angles and perpendicular bisectors); Form 1 Chapter 10 (perimeter and area); Form 1 Chapter 13 (Pythagoras' theorem); fractions and squares.

## Construction and investigation requirements

### 5.1 Constructions from given conditions
- A construction prompt must state all determining conditions and the permitted instruments. Do not depend on an approximately drawn figure to supply a length or angle.
- Core construction cases are:
  1. a circle with a given radius or diameter;
  2. a diameter through a specified point in a circle when the centre is given;
  3. a chord of given length through a specified point on the circumference; and
  4. a sector with a given radius and central angle.
- The answer model should preserve arcs and construction lines when they are evidence of the method. Assess accuracy and the rationale for key steps, not only the appearance of the final figure.

### 5.2 Symmetry and locating the centre
- Treat every diameter as an axis of symmetry of the circle. Reflection in that diameter also explains the equal halves of any chord perpendicular to it.
- Encode both directions of each converse pair. For example, a radius perpendicular to a chord bisects it, while a radius through the chord's midpoint is perpendicular to it.
- To locate the centre of an unmarked circle, generate two distinct, non-parallel chords. Construct the perpendicular bisector of each chord; their unique intersection is the centre. The radius is then the distance from this point to any point on the circumference.
- Avoid chords whose perpendicular bisectors are coincident or nearly parallel in the rendered diagram. Independently verify that the constructed intersection is equidistant from sampled points on the circle.
- For equal-chord distance tasks, distance from the centre to a chord means the length of the perpendicular segment. Encode both directions:
  - equal chords are equidistant from the centre; and
  - chords equidistant from the centre are equal in length.
- Likewise encode both directions for equal chords and equal arcs. Do not import Form 3 angle-at-the-centre or angle-at-the-circumference theorems into a core Form 2 item.

### 5.3 Deriving the formulae
- **Circumference:** provide several measured or generated circles, calculate `C/d`, and use the approximately constant ratio to introduce `π`. Generalise `C = πd`, then substitute `d = 2r` to obtain `C = 2πr`. If measurement data are used, label ratios as approximate and allow for stated measurement tolerance.
- **Area:** divide a circle into equal sectors and rearrange alternating sectors. As the number of sectors increases, the shape approaches a rectangle/parallelogram with height `r` and base equal to half the circumference, `πr`. Hence `A = (πr)(r) = πr²`. Diagrams in a finite-sector investigation must describe the rearranged shape as an approximation rather than an exact rectangle.
- Derive arc length and sector area as the fraction `θ/360°` of the whole circumference and area respectively. The central angle and the selected arc/sector must match in the diagram's internal geometry.
- A formula-recall or application item may follow an earlier derivation, but the generator must also produce explanation, generalisation and investigation items so the learning standard is not reduced to substitution.

## Difficulty strategies

### 5.1 Properties and constructions
- **Easy**: label parts; use `d = 2r`; construct a circle of a stated radius.
- **Medium**: construct a diameter through a given point or a chord of given length through a point on the circumference; explain one key step.
- **Hard**: construct a sector from conditions or complete a multi-step construction whose centre or radius must first be determined. Difficulty must come from reasoning within the stated construction standards, not from later-form circle theorems.

### 5.2 Symmetrical properties of chords
- **Easy**: identify a diameter as an axis of symmetry; use a perpendicular radius to identify equal half-chords.
- **Medium**: construct perpendicular bisectors of two chords to locate the centre; apply either direction of an equal-chord/equal-distance or equal-chord/equal-arc relationship.
- **Hard**: combine two chord properties, or find a radius from chord length and perpendicular distance using Pythagoras' theorem. Generate the circle, chord and perpendicular foot from one consistent geometric model.
- Distractors: treating a non-perpendicular centre-to-chord segment as the distance; assuming unequal chords are equidistant; using only one chord's perpendicular bisector as if it uniquely fixes the centre in the plane.

### 5.3 Circumference, area, arcs and sectors
- **Easy**: generalise `C = πd` from a `C/d` table; find circumference or area for a given radius; find the area of a semicircle or quadrant.
- **Medium**: explain the sector-rearrangement area derivation; determine arc length or sector area for a simple central angle; reverse a circumference or area calculation; include the diameter in the perimeter of a semicircle.
- **Hard**: solve a composite perimeter or area problem; determine `θ` from arc length or sector area; solve a rolling-wheel, coverage or cost problem with explicit units and rounding instructions.
- Distractors: omitting straight edges from a composite perimeter; confusing radius and diameter; using the full-circle formula for a sector; calculating an annulus as `π(R-r)²`; rounding intermediate values prematurely; not following the stated value of `π`.

## Visual aids
- **REQUIRED** for constructions, chord-symmetry deductions, formula derivations and all composite or shaded-region questions. Store the exact centre, radius, chord endpoints, perpendicular feet, central angles and region boundaries in the diagram model.
- Construction diagrams should display relevant construction arcs/lines and be accurately printable at a controlled scale when measurement is assessed.
- Deduction diagrams need only be constraint-consistent and may be marked **not drawn to scale**. Do not make near-scale appearance the evidence for equality, perpendicularity or bisection; use markings and given statements.
- Simple numerical circumference or area questions may be text-only. For every visual, include labels, units, alt text, intended print size and collision checks.

## Generator notes
- State the value of `π` to use when a numerical approximation is required, or request an exact answer in terms of `π`. Do not write equality between an exact expression and a rounded decimal.
- Select values backward from a validated answer. If using `π = 22/7` for a clean numerical result, choose compatible dimensions; if using `3.142`, specify the required rounding.
- Useful central angles include `30°`, `36°`, `40°`, `45°`, `60°`, `72°`, `90°`, `120°`, `135°` and `150°`, but angle choice should serve the intended reasoning rather than guarantee an integer answer.
- Composite examples may include a rectangle with semicircular ends, a square with an inscribed circle, or an annulus. Treat these as possible contexts, not as claims about examination frequency.
- Independently recompute all lengths and areas from the internal geometric model, check that every requested construction is possible, and validate units, rounding and answer uniqueness before rendering.
- Cross-link only to mastered content: Form 1 Chapter 10 for composite perimeter/area, Form 1 Chapter 13 for right triangles in chord problems, and Form 3 Chapter 6 only when an item is explicitly tagged as later-form cumulative practice.
