# Form 1, Chapter 6: Linear Equations

## Overview
Introduces equality and linear equations in one variable and in two variables. Students solve one-variable equations by maintaining equality, form and represent equations of the form `ax + by = c`, and recognise ordered-pair solutions. The chapter also introduces simultaneous linear equations in two variables, including their graphical interpretation and solution by graph, substitution, and elimination, followed by contextual problem solving and verification.

## Prerequisites
Algebraic expressions (Ch5); integer/fraction operations (Ch1); plotting ordered pairs and reading Cartesian coordinates, including all four quadrants.

| # | Subtopic | Content coverage | Key skills / formulae | Typical question types |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Linear equations and equalities | Meaning of equation, unknown, coefficient, constant, and solution; linear vs non-linear equations; verifying a solution by substitution; writing equations from statements and situations. | A solution makes the equality true. Preserve equality by performing the same valid operation on both sides. | Identify linear equations; check whether a given value or ordered pair is a solution; form an equation from words. |
| 6.2 | Linear equations in one variable | Solve `x ± a = b`, `ax = b`, `x/a = b`, `ax ± b = c`, `a(x ± b) = c`, `ax ± b = cx ± d`, and equations with fractional coefficients; solve problems by forming an equation and then solving it. | Balance method / inverse operations; clear denominators using a common multiple. | Solve a given equation; find an unknown in a perimeter, age, quantity, or money situation. |
| 6.3 | Linear equations in two variables | Meaning and general form `ax + by = c`, where `a` and `b` are not both zero; form equations from statements, tables, and situations; determine and verify possible ordered-pair solutions. | A solution is an ordered pair `(x, y)` satisfying the equation. One non-degenerate linear equation in two variables has infinitely many real ordered-pair solutions. | Form `ax + by = c`; complete a table of values; test or generate ordered-pair solutions; find one coordinate when the other is known. |

## Difficulty strategies

### 6.1 Equations and equality
- **Easy**: identify which of four equations is linear; verify `x = 3` in `2x + 1 = 7`.
- **Medium**: form an equation from a one-sentence description.
- **Hard**: form an equation from geometric or comparison contexts, such as angles on a line or algebraic side lengths.

### 6.2 Solving equations in one variable
- **Easy**: one or two steps with integer answers (`x + 5 = 12`, `3x = 21`, `2x - 3 = 9`).
- **Medium**: variable on both sides (`5x - 4 = 2x + 8`); brackets (`3(x - 2) = 15`); negative or fractional answers; equations with fractions (`x/3 + 2 = 5`).
- **Hard**: fractional equations with different denominators (`(x + 1)/2 = (2x - 3)/5`); word problems requiring formulation and solution; answers that are fractions requiring simplification; equations where the variable has a negative coefficient on one side.
- Distractors: moving a term without changing its sign; applying an operation to only part of one side; mishandling a negative coefficient or denominator; translating “three more than” as multiplication.

### 6.3 Equations and ordered pairs in two variables
- **Easy**: test one ordered pair in `2x + y = 7`; find `y` for a given `x`.
- **Medium**: complete a table containing positive, zero, and negative coordinates; form an equation such as `3x + 2y = 18` from a quantity/cost statement.
- **Hard**: generate several valid integer ordered pairs under stated bounds; determine an unknown coefficient from a known solution; translate a less-direct context while defining both variables clearly.
- Distractors: reversing the order of `(x, y)`; substituting a coordinate into the wrong variable; treating one equation in two variables as if it had only one solution.

### 6.4 Graphs
- **Easy**: plot three supplied points and join them with a straight line; recognise `x = 3` as vertical and `y = -2` as horizontal.
- **Medium**: construct a table and graph a line that crosses more than one quadrant; determine intercepts or missing coordinates from the graph.
- **Hard**: compare two lines on the same axes, including a horizontal or vertical line; recover an equation from labelled points; use awkward but readable scales without making graphical answers ambiguous.
- Do not make first-quadrant-only graphs the default. Sample equations and axis ranges so positive and negative coordinates, axes, and the origin all occur across the item bank.

### 6.5-6.6 Simultaneous equations and solution methods
- **Easy**: identify the intersection of two clearly drawn lines; solve a system where substitution is immediate, such as `y = x + 1` and `x + y = 7`.
- **Medium**: use elimination when one pair of coefficients is already equal/opposite; rearrange one equation before substitution; classify a displayed pair as one, no, or infinitely many solutions.
- **Hard**: multiply one or both equations before elimination; solve systems with negative or fractional solutions; choose and justify an efficient method; distinguish near-parallel lines without relying on visual appearance alone.
- Include all three solution cases. Generate parallel systems with proportional variable coefficients but inconsistent constants, and coincident systems with all coefficients and constants proportional.
- Distractors: eliminating unequal coefficients; changing a sign incorrectly after multiplying an equation; reporting separate values that do not form one ordered pair; accepting a point that satisfies only one equation.

### 6.7 Contextual problems
- **Easy**: two clearly stated totals with small positive-integer answers, with variables already defined.
- **Medium**: students define the variables and form both equations before solving; units or labels must be attached to the final values.
- **Hard**: multi-sentence contexts with information presented in a table or diagram, non-unit coefficients, and a final interpretation or comparison. Additional wording may increase representation demand, but every supplied fact must be relevant or explicitly identified as contextual background.
- Contexts should lead to two independent linear relationships and a meaningful admissible solution. Avoid artificial fractional people/items unless the quantities are continuous.

## Visual aids
- **NONE** for pure algebraic solving and short formulation/verification items.
- **RECOMMENDED** for a table-to-graph task or when students need to read a graphical intersection.
- **REQUIRED** when the graph itself supplies coordinates, intercepts, line relationships, or an approximate simultaneous-equation solution.
- Use axes with arrowheads, origin, variable labels, units where applicable, consistent and explicitly marked intervals on each axis, and all quadrants required by the generated coordinates. Label each line unambiguously.
- Render vertical lines exactly as `x = k` and horizontal lines exactly as `y = k`; do not infer either from a finite sloping segment.
- Keep the mathematical coordinate model separate from the rendered SVG/image so plotted points, line equations, labels, and the answer key are derived from the same data.

## Generator notes
- Preserve one-variable coverage: choose coefficients so Easy/Medium answers are usually small integers; Hard answers may include simplified fractions.
- For `ax + by = c`, require `(a, b) != (0, 0)`. Normalise equations only when equivalent forms would otherwise make marking ambiguous; accept non-zero scalar multiples where equation-form answers are allowed.
- Generate ordered pairs first, then derive constants, or independently substitute every proposed pair to prevent invalid answer choices.
- For a graph, choose the equation, window, and scale together. Ensure at least two distinct visible solution points per non-vertical line and avoid requiring students to estimate an intersection more precisely than the grid supports.
- When graphical solving is assessed, state that the answer is approximate if the intersection is not exactly on a marked grid point and define an acceptable tolerance.
- Generate unique-solution systems from a chosen solution `(p, q)` and a non-zero determinant `a1*b2 - a2*b1`. Independently solve the generated system before rendering it.
- For systems `a1*x + b1*y = c1` and `a2*x + b2*y = c2`, use cross-products rather than ratio division when classifying dependent or inconsistent equations. This safely handles zero coefficients: coefficient vectors are proportional for parallel lines, while the constants are proportional as well only for coincident lines.
- Algebraic-method questions should retain the original equations for final verification. Substitute the ordered pair into **both** equations and check both equalities exactly.
- Context generators must check variable domains (for example, non-negative counts, integers for indivisible objects, positive lengths/prices), units, uniqueness, and whether the interpreted answer addresses the question asked.
- Age, consecutive-number, perimeter, quantity, and money templates may be parameterised, but do not label them as guaranteed or high-frequency without a dated assessment corpus.
