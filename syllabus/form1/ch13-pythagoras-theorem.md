# Form 1, Chapter 13: The Pythagoras Theorem

## Overview
Introduces the Pythagoras theorem for right-angled triangles and its converse. Students find unknown sides, verify right angles, and solve contextual problems (ladders, cables, distances, diagonals of rectangles). It is a foundation for Form 3 trigonometry and later coordinate-geometry distance ideas.

## Prerequisites
Squares and square roots (Ch3); triangles (Ch9).

## Topic & subtopic breakdown

| # | Subtopic | Content coverage | Key skills / formulae | Typical question types |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 13.1 | The Pythagoras theorem | Identify hypotenuse; theorem a² + b² = c²; find hypotenuse; find a leg; Pythagorean triples (3-4-5, 5-12-13, 6-8-10, 8-15-17, 7-24-25 and multiples); diagonal of square/rectangle; composite right-triangle problems. | c = √(a² + b²); a = √(c² − b²). | Find missing side; find diagonal; two-triangle diagrams sharing a side; word problems (ladder, supporting cable, guy rope). |
| 13.2 | The converse of the Pythagoras theorem | Verify whether three lengths can form a triangle; identify the longest side as the candidate hypotenuse; verify whether the triangle is right-angled; identify the right-angle position; applications (checking square corners, construction). | For valid side lengths a ≤ b ≤ c, if a² + b² = c², then the triangle is right-angled opposite c. | Yes/no with working for three given sides; which vertex is the right angle. |

## Difficulty strategies

### 13.1
- **Easy**: Pythagorean triple, find hypotenuse (legs 6, 8 → c = 10).
- **Medium**: find a leg (c = 13, one leg 5 → other 12); non-triple with integer answer after computation (legs 9, 12); decimal-friendly sides.
- **Hard**: non-perfect-square results requiring decimal rounding to specified d.p.; composite diagrams (two right triangles sharing a leg — find a length requiring two applications); rectangle diagonal plus perimeter/area follow-on; word problems where students must model (ladder against wall, distance across a park, shortest path).
- Distractors: adding instead of subtracting when finding a leg; misidentifying the hypotenuse (angle marker must be checked); forgetting the square root (answering c²).

### 13.2 Converse
- **Easy**: verify 3-4-5.
- **Medium**: scaled triples (9-12-15; 0.5-1.2-1.3); identify longest side first.
- **Hard**: near-miss triples (5-11-12? No) requiring exact comparison; decimal sides; converse embedded in a construction/roof-frame word problem. All supplied lengths must first satisfy the triangle inequality.
- Distractors: comparing a² + b² with c instead of c²; not using the longest side as candidate hypotenuse.

## Visual aids
- **REQUIRED** — labelled right-triangle SVG with right-angle square marker, side labels, and the unknown marked (e.g. x). For suitable word problems, use a simple scene diagram such as a wall and ladder.
- Composite figure questions need the full diagram.

## Generator notes
- Prefer generating from Pythagorean triples (with scale factor k) for clean answers at Easy/Medium; at Hard use sides giving non-integer hypotenuse and specify rounding ("correct to 2 decimal places").
- The hypotenuse must always be opposite the right-angle marker — enforce in generated diagrams.
- For every three-side instance, sort the positive lengths as a ≤ b ≤ c and reject it unless a + b > c. In a converse item, test a² + b² against c² only after identifying c as the longest side. If equality holds, place the right angle opposite c; otherwise do not label the triangle as right-angled.
- Common real contexts: ladders, supporting cables or guy ropes, televisions ("screen size" diagonal), rectangular field diagonals, and direct displacements represented by a supplied right triangle.
- Avoid shadow-height contexts unless the question directly supplies the two perpendicular side lengths of a single right triangle. Inferring a height from a shadow and a separate reference triangle requires similar triangles or trigonometry and is outside this chapter's core method.
- Cross-link: Form 3 Ch5 trigonometry (right-triangle side/angle relations), Form 2 Ch7 coordinate distance between two points, Form 2 Ch6 (3D diagonals of cuboids at recognition level).
