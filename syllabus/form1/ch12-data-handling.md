# Form 1, Chapter 12: Data Handling

## Overview
Introduction to statistics through the complete statistical-inquiry cycle: formulate a statistical question, select and collect relevant data, organise and represent the data, analyse and interpret the representation, draw inferences or make predictions, and communicate conclusions ethically. Core representations are frequency tables, bar charts, pie charts, line graphs, dot plots, stem-and-leaf plots, histograms and frequency polygons. This chapter is the foundation of the statistics line (Form 2 Ch12, Form 4 Ch8 and Form 5 Ch7).

Pictographs may be used only as **supporting prerequisite practice** from primary mathematics, not as a core Form 1 learning outcome. Histogram and frequency-polygon work here concerns conversion and interpretation of ungrouped data representations; grouped-data measures and grouped-dispersion analysis belong to later forms.

## Prerequisites
Arithmetic with integers and decimals; fraction and percentage basics (primary school); reading simple tables and pictographs (primary school); angles for pie charts (Ch8 helps).

## Difficulty strategies

Difficulty must come from reasoning, representation shifts, justification and reduced scaffolding—not from importing grouped-data measures or later-form content.

### 12.1 Statistical inquiry
- **Easy**: identify which of several questions is statistical; classify a familiar variable as categorical/numerical or discrete/continuous; place named inquiry stages in order.
- **Medium**: rewrite a leading or ambiguous survey question; identify the required population, variable and data-collection method; complete a partially specified inquiry plan.
- **Hard**: generate a statistical question for a context, design the full inquiry cycle, anticipate variability or bias, and state how the results will be represented and communicated. Require a justification for important design choices.

### 12.2–12.3 Representation and conversion
- **Easy**: read one value; tally a short raw list; complete a single missing frequency; construct a labelled bar chart, dot plot or stem-and-leaf plot from a small data set.
- **Medium**: convert a frequency table to a pie chart, histogram or frequency polygon; convert a graph back to a table; compare two representations; complete a partially drawn graph after calculating missing values.
- **Hard**: perform two representation shifts (for example raw data → frequency table → histogram/frequency polygon), verify that totals and frequencies are preserved, and justify which representation best answers a stated question. Missing-frequency tasks may combine a total with pie angles or graph values, but must have a unique solution.
- **Pictograph prerequisite items only**: reading a simple key or fractional symbol may check prior knowledge, but do not use pictographs as the main Form 1 difficulty progression or label them core.
- Common errors/distractors: confusing pie percentage with sector angle; treating histogram bars as categories; separating histogram bars without reason; plotting frequency-polygon points at inconsistent horizontal positions; ignoring a stem-and-leaf key; changing the total during conversion; reading the wrong series or axis.

### 12.4 Interpretation, inference and prediction
- **Easy**: state a maximum, minimum, total or directly visible trend.
- **Medium**: compare categories or distributions using two cited values; distinguish a direct observation from an inference; interpolate within a stable time trend.
- **Hard**: evaluate a claim, make and qualify a prediction, or explain why the evidence is insufficient. Include plausible but unsupported causal or overgeneralised claims as distractors.

### 12.5 Ethical representation
- **Easy**: spot a missing title, unit, label or key.
- **Medium**: explain how a truncated baseline, reversed/unequal scale, omitted category or unrepresentative sample changes the reader's impression; redraw or specify a correction.
- **Hard**: audit both collection and display. A valid item may combine a leading question or sampling bias with a graph whose area/length encoding, axis or labelling is misleading, then ask for a defensible correction and conclusion.

## Visual aids
- **REQUIRED** when the representation itself supplies information or is being constructed, converted, compared or audited: bar chart, pie chart, line graph, dot plot, stem-and-leaf plot, histogram or frequency polygon.
- **OPTIONAL** for statistical-question design, inquiry-cycle planning, data-type classification and other tasks whose evidence is completely provided in text or a table.
- Pictographs may appear only in explicitly tagged prerequisite items and must include a key.
- All graphical assets must include title/context, axis labels, units, readable scale markings and a text-equivalent data table/model. A deliberately flawed graph must still be legible, and the intended flaw must be recorded in metadata.
- Bar-chart categories normally have separated bars. Histogram bars touch and must represent a numerical frequency distribution consistently. Do not introduce frequency density or unequal class-width calculations here.
- Render a frequency polygon from the same underlying frequencies as its paired table/histogram. Define the horizontal positions used and handle endpoints consistently; do not add artificial endpoint conventions unless stated.

## Generator notes

### Inquiry and content scope
- Generate the statistical question before generating data. It must identify or imply the population/group, variable and context, admit varying answers, and be answerable by the proposed data.
- Store the inquiry as structured stages: `question`, `population_or_respondents`, `variable`, `collection_method`, `raw_data`, `organisation`, `representation`, `analysis`, `inference_or_prediction`, and `communication`.
- Keep histogram and frequency-polygon questions to organising, converting, reading and comparing suitable ungrouped data. Do not ask for grouped mean, variance, standard deviation, cumulative frequency, ogive, frequency density or unequal-class-width analysis.
- Treat pictographs as `scope: prerequisite`, not `scope: core`.

### Representation generation and validation
- Generate one canonical data model first, and derive every table, chart, prompt and answer from it. Never independently invent graph labels and answer values.
- Keep totals convenient when clean construction is intended: for example, 36, 60, 120 or 360 for integer pie angles. If rounding is assessed, state the rounding rule and validate that the displayed sectors still form 360° subject to that rule.
- For stem-and-leaf plots, order leaves and include a key such as “`4 | 7` means 47”. For split stems, explicitly state the convention.
- For every conversion, validate that categories/value positions match, all frequencies are non-negative integers where counts are intended, and the total frequency is unchanged. Reconstruct the source table from the rendered graph and compare it with the canonical model.
- For histograms, reject categorical horizontal axes, unintended gaps/overlaps, inconsistent interval boundaries and any task that silently requires frequency density. For frequency polygons, reject points whose horizontal positions or frequencies do not match the specified distribution.
- Choose representation for purpose: bar chart for comparing categories, pie chart for parts of a whole, line graph for change over an ordered/time variable, dot plot or stem-and-leaf plot for retaining individual values, and histogram/frequency polygon for the shape of a numerical frequency distribution. Accept another representation only when the student's data-specific justification is valid.

### Inference, prediction and ethics validation
- An inference or prediction answer must cite at least one correct feature/value from the data and remain within the stated population and context. Flag causal language, certainty from a short trend, and generalisation beyond the sample as invalid unless the item supplies evidence that warrants it.
- Misleading-graph generation must deliberately vary exactly the intended feature(s), then test that no accidental second flaw changes the answer. Validate at least:
  - axis baseline (especially a truncated vertical axis for bar/length comparisons);
  - scale order and equal spacing for equal increments; unequal or broken scales must be explicit;
  - length encoding proportional to value and, when symbols/regions encode magnitude, area—not merely width—proportional to value;
  - complete title, category/value labels, units, legend/key, time interval and source/context where relevant;
  - every category/data point included and totals consistent;
  - neutral question wording and a sampling method/group that can support the claimed population.
- A non-zero baseline is not automatically unethical for every line graph. Judge whether it materially distorts the intended comparison, and require the axis to be clearly marked.
- Do not label a conclusion “the graph proves …” when it only describes the collected sample. Ethical communication distinguishes data, inference, prediction and opinion.

### Answer and rendering checks
- Solve every generated item independently from the canonical data and compare with the stored answer.
- Ensure the requested inference or justification is gradable: store required evidence points and accepted equivalent wording, not only one model sentence.
- Test charts at intended print size for tick-label collisions, clipped legends, distinguishable series and readable grayscale output; supply semantic labels/alt text and the underlying table.
- Do not describe any chart type as a guaranteed or high-frequency SPM item without a dated, analysed paper corpus.
