# Form 1, Chapter 2: Factors and Multiples

## Overview
Develops number theory basics: factors, multiples, prime numbers, prime factorisation, common factors/multiples, HCF and LCM. Feeds directly into fraction arithmetic (Ch1), algebraic factorisation (Form 2 Ch2), and indices (Form 3 Ch1).

## Prerequisites
Multiplication tables to 12; division with remainders; integer operations from Ch1.

## Topic & subtopic breakdown

| # | Subtopic | Content coverage | Key skills / formulae | Typical question types |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Factors, prime factors and multiples | Meaning of factor and multiple; listing factors/multiples; prime numbers (identify ≤ 100); prime factorisation (factor tree, repeated division); expressing a number as a product of prime factors in index notation. | Factor tree; index notation a^m × b^n. | List factors; is n prime?; write 360 = 2³×3²×5; find missing prime factor. |
| 2.2 | Common factors and HCF | Common factors of two/three numbers; HCF via listing, prime factorisation, or division algorithm; solving simple problems using HCF (equal grouping, largest square tiles). | HCF = product of lowest powers of common primes. | Find HCF of (a, b) or (a, b, c); application word problems. |
| 2.3 | Common multiples and LCM | Common multiples of two/three numbers; LCM via listing or prime factorisation; problems using LCM (events repeating together, bell-ringing, smallest equal share). | LCM = product of highest powers of all primes. | Find LCM; "two bells ring every x and y minutes…" problems. |

## Difficulty strategies

### 2.1
- **Easy**: numbers ≤ 50; single prime factors; list factors of 24.
- **Medium**: numbers 50–500; prime factorisation with 3 distinct primes.
- **Hard**: numbers up to ~1000; index notation required; reverse problems ("given 2^a × 3^b × 5 = 360, find a and b").
- Distractors: counting 1 or the number itself wrongly; use composites such as 51 or 91 as plausible prime-identification distractors (do not call them "pseudo-primes", which has a different technical meaning in number theory).

### 2.2 HCF
- **Easy**: two numbers ≤ 50, one or two common factors.
- **Medium**: two numbers ≤ 200 or three small numbers; prime-factorisation method needed.
- **Hard**: three numbers ≤ 500; word problems (largest equal groups of mixed items; square tiles covering a rectangle exactly) requiring the student to *recognise* that HCF is the relevant concept.

### 2.3 LCM
- **Easy**: two numbers ≤ 20.
- **Medium**: two numbers ≤ 100 or three numbers ≤ 30.
- **Hard**: word problems where LCM must be inferred (bus schedules, flashing lights, "smallest number divisible by…"); combined HCF+LCM problems. For exactly two positive integers `a` and `b`, verification may use `HCF(a,b) × LCM(a,b) = ab`; do not extend this identity to three or more integers.
- Distractors: confusing HCF with LCM; arithmetic slips in prime factorisation; forgetting all primes when three numbers used.

## Visual aids
- 2.1: **RECOMMENDED** — factor tree diagram (SVG) for prime factorisation questions; a 10×10 grid (Sieve of Eratosthenes style) for prime-identification questions.
- 2.2/2.3: **OPTIONAL** — simple diagrams for tiling/grouping word problems (rectangle to tile with squares); otherwise NONE.

## Generator notes
- Keep a curated list of plausible prime-identification distractors (for example, the composite numbers 51, 57, 87 and 91). Do not label these numbers "pseudo-primes".
- Restrict the identity `HCF(a,b) × LCM(a,b) = ab` to two positive integers. For questions involving three or more integers, calculate HCF and LCM directly rather than applying this two-integer identity.
- For word problems, HCF questions involve *dividing/splitting equally into the largest possible* groups; LCM questions involve *events coinciding* or *smallest common quantity*.
- Ensure generated numbers have reasonable prime factorisations (primes ≤ 13) unless difficulty explicitly allows larger.
- Cross-link: prime factorisation is reused in Form 3 indices simplification; HCF/LCM appears in fraction addition contexts.
